The Weight of Ordinary Things

A teacup, a house key, a coat left on a hook. Mercer finds whole lives in the things we stop noticing.

Buy on Amazon ↗ All retailers

Synopsis

In twenty-two short essays, Adrian W. Mercer turns his attention to the objects and rituals we stop seeing: the spare key, the Sunday roast, the coat that stays on its hook for a year after its owner has gone.

Moving between memoir, social history, and philosophy, he asks why some things grow heavier the longer we keep them, and what our kitchens, drawers, and mantelpieces remember on our behalf.

Tender, exact, and quietly funny, The Weight of Ordinary Things is a book about paying attention, and about the lives hidden in plain sight.

Excerpt · From the essay “On Keys”

There is a key on my desk that opens nothing. It belonged to my grandmother’s front door, a door that was taken off its hinges in 1997 when the house was sold, gutted, and made, in the language of the brochure, “fit for modern living.” I have carried the key through four moves and two decades. It has no lock to return to. By any practical measure, it is rubbish.

And yet I cannot throw it away, because a key is not really an object. It is a promise that somewhere there is a door that will let you in. Hold it long enough and you can feel the weight of the hallway behind it: the coats on their hooks, the smell of coal tar soap, the clock that gained four minutes a week and was never corrected because everyone in the house had learned to subtract them.

We think of history as something that happens in parliaments and on battlefields. Most of it happens in kitchens. Most of it is carried in pockets.
