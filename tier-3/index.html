The memory

The son of a clockmaker and a librarian, Adrian W. Mercer spent ten years measuring empty houses before he started writing about the people who left them.

His novels and essays ask how personal histories shape collective memory, and how ordinary moments reveal extraordinary truths.

No. 02 · Wren’s End, winter 1953

A house is closed. The clocks are stopped.

Nobody says why. The doors are locked, the curtains drawn, and the house begins to keep its own counsel.

Sixty years later

The table is still laid for four.

Eleanor Hale opens the door on dust so even and so soft it looks deliberate, as though the room was dressed for a photograph nobody took.

Under the stairs

Two hundred and twelve letters.

A biscuit tin. Every letter addressed, stamped, and sealed. Not one of them posted.

The Architecture of Silence
Chapter One · Sample

The house at Wren’s End had been waiting for sixty years, and it had not tidied up.

Eleanor found the table laid for four. Plates, cups turned down on their saucers, a butter dish with its lid on, all of it under a fur of dust so even and so soft it looked deliberate, as though someone had dressed the room for a photograph and then forgotten to take it. The clock on the mantel had stopped at ten to six. Outside, the garden had grown up to the sills and pressed its leaves against the glass, as if it, too, wanted to see what happened next.

She had been told the house was empty. It was not empty. It was full to the ceiling with the particular silence of rooms in which something has been left unsaid.
