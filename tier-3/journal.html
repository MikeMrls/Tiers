The Stories Hidden Inside Old Buildings

+

Every old building is a ledger nobody has finished reading. The surveyor sees the obvious entries: the Victorian extension, the bricked-up window, the staircase moved in 1930 to make room for a bathroom. The more interesting entries are smaller. A doorframe worn pale at exactly the height of a child’s hand. Pencil marks on a pantry wall, each one dated, rising a few inches a year until 1941, where they stop.

I spent ten years measuring rooms for a living, and I learned that walls are honest in a way people rarely are. They do not edit. They simply accumulate. The work of the historian, and I think of the novelist too, is to stand in a room long enough to notice what it has been trying to tell you.

— A.

Jul 30, 2026

On Memory and the Places We Leave Behind

+

There is a particular kind of grief reserved for places that still exist but are no longer yours. The house is there; somebody else’s car is in the drive. The school is there, renamed after a donor. You can walk past them. You can even, if you are brave or foolish, knock on the door.

What you cannot do is go back. Memory is not a recording. It is a map we redraw every time we consult it, and every redrawing loses a street or two. I used to think the point of writing about places was to preserve them. Now I think it is to be honest about the redrawing, and to admit which streets I have invented to fill the gaps.

— A.

Why Ordinary Things Deserve Extraordinary Attention

+

My grandmother kept a drawer of string. Not balls of it, but individual pieces, each saved from a parcel and wound around two fingers into a neat figure of eight. When she died we found more than three hundred. Nobody had ever needed that much string. That was never the point.

We are trained to look for meaning in the remarkable: the wedding, the war, the letter that changed everything. But most of a life is made of drawers like that one, small habits repeated until they become a kind of self-portrait. If you want to know who someone was, skip the photographs. Open the kitchen drawers.

— A.

The Quiet Disappearance of Independent Bookshops

+

Bookshops rarely close with a bang. They close with a sign in the window, then a sale, then a week of people saying they had always meant to come in more often. The last time I watched it happen, the owner gave the shelving away to anyone who could carry it. A man left with a whole bay of poetry on his shoulder, books still in it.

What goes is not just the stock. It is the conversation at the till, the recommendation no algorithm would have found, the noticeboard with a lost cat and a choir looking for tenors. A bookshop is one of the few places left where you can spend an hour without being asked to explain yourself. We should notice that before it becomes another pin on the map of things we used to have.

— A.

Feb 9, 2026

Reading History Through the Lives of Strangers

+

The most useful archive I ever found was a box of other people’s postcards, bought for a pound at a church sale. Nobody famous. A woman writing from the seaside in 1952 to say the weather had turned and Harold had lost his hat. A boy at camp who wanted his mother to know the food was terrible and he was having the time of his life.

Official history tells you what happened. Strangers tell you what it felt like. I have come to trust the second kind more, not because it is more accurate, but because it is less interested in being remembered. Nobody writes about a lost hat for posterity. That is exactly why it survives.

— A.
